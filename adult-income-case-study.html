Predicting who earns over $50K, and knowing how far to trust the answer

A classification project on the Adult Census Income data (48,842 rows). The goal was not just a high score. It was a pipeline where nothing leaks, the test set is used once, and results are checked for men and women separately.

The problem

Given a person's age, education, job, marital status, hours worked and capital gains, predict whether their income is above $50K. About 24% of people are in the positive class, so accuracy alone is misleading: predicting "under $50K" for everyone is already 76% accurate. I used PR-AUC as the main metric, where a model with no skill scores about 0.24.

What was hiding in the data

The raw file reports zero missing values, but missing data is stored as the string "?". It appears in workclass, occupation (about 5.7% of rows each) and native country (about 1.8%). Those rows are not random: people with an unknown job have a different rate of high income than people with a known one, so I kept "Unknown" as its own category rather than dropping rows or filling with the most common value.

Other findings that shaped the design:

  • Capital gain is zero for 92% of people and heavily skewed. The value 99,999 is a cap used by the survey: 244 rows, all earning over $50K. I used log1p plus a separate "has gain" flag.
  • Hours per week spikes at round numbers (22,803 people report exactly 40), so it is better treated as approximate.
  • Education is stored twice (a label and a number), and marital status overlaps heavily with relationship. I kept the numeric education only.
  • 52 exact duplicate rows were removed before splitting, leaving 48,790.

How I kept the evaluation honest

  • Split first. 80/20 train/test (39,032 / 9,758). The modelling code only ever loads training rows.
  • No leakage. Scaling and one-hot encoding live inside a scikit-learn Pipeline, so they are refitted on each cross-validation fold's training part only. A check also confirms the target never enters the features.
  • An append-only experiment log. Every run, its settings and its out-of-fold predictions are saved, and nothing is overwritten.
  • The test set is locked. The final script refuses to run without a confirmation flag and refuses to run twice.

Model selection

I screened nine models with untuned defaults on 5-fold cross-validation: logistic regression, naive Bayes, decision tree, linear SVM, histogram gradient boosting, random forest, k-nearest neighbours, a small neural network and an RBF SVM. The two best, gradient boosting and random forest, went on to a small grid search. Tuning used plain cross-validation rather than nested, so I treat the tuned scores as slightly optimistic. The final choice was default gradient boosting, decided on training data only.

Choosing a threshold

The usual 0.5 cut-off is a convention, and for a 24% positive class it is rarely best. Using out-of-fold predictions from three different fold shuffles, I picked 0.37, the value that maximised F1. In cross-validation it gave:

Limitations

  • The data is from a 1994 census, so the income threshold and the patterns do not describe today.
  • Sex, race and country are in the data. This is a modelling exercise, not something to use for real decisions about people.
  • Tuning was not nested, and the fold shuffles reuse the same training rows.
